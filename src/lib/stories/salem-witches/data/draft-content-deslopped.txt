Every accusation of the Salem witch trials, drawn as a network. **412 people** appear in the record and **1,096 accusations** connect them. Each arrow runs from the person who made an accusation to the person they named, a directed graph of who pointed at whom between February 1692 and January 1693.

From a distance it is a tangle. The tangle has an order, and one structural fact explains why this record can be read as a chain of courtrooms and grudges or as a **contagion**. This story builds toward that fact.

Start with the raw graph, all 412 people and every arrow between them. The accused cluster together, the accusers fan out across them, and a few names sit at the center of nearly everything.

Most people accuse a handful of times, many not at all. A handful accuse dozens of times. Highlighted here are the **six most prolific accusers**: Mary Walcott (79 accusations), Ann Putnam Jr. (77), Abigail Williams (62), Mary Warren (59), Mercy Lewis (59), and Elizabeth Hubbard (54).

Together these six people account for **389 of the 1,096 accusations**, more than a third of the entire network. An outbreak driven by **superspreaders** looks like this: a small number of sources carrying most of the transmission.

A contagion recruits as it spreads. Highlighted in red are **51 people who switch sides**: they appear first among the accusers and the afflicted, and later stand accused themselves. Among them are Tituba, Mary Warren, Abigail Williams, and Martha Corey.

The record names the mechanism. **95 accusations** describe the same act, the signing of the Devil's book. The network recruits from within its own victims, which is how a spreading process replenishes itself.

Here is the structural fact at the center of this story: **the network has no loops.**

Follow any chain of accusations and you can never find your way back to where you started. The only exceptions are **seven people who appear to themselves**, the accused seen in their own dreams, drawn here as small rings. Apart from those seven, the 1,096 accusations contain no cycle.

Laid out by generation, **267 pure accusers** sit at the root, with accusations passing downward through as many as **five steps**. Every arrow points away from the root.

A network with no loops and a recruitment step is what a **witch-recruitment network, or an epidemic, looks like**. The "infection" cannot cycle back on itself; it only moves forward to new people.

Because the structure is a contagion, we can fit **classic epidemiological models** to the data, the same models used for the spread of disease.

## The curve of an outbreak

If the accusations behave like a contagion, they should leave a contagion's trace in time. Here is every one of the 1,096 accusations, dated to the month it was made.

Each bar is one month of accusations, from the first in February 1692 to the last in January 1693. Watch the outbreak accelerate through the spring.

It crests in **May 1692**, when **290 accusations** are made in a single month, the high-water mark of the crisis.

Then it falls away through the summer, until a **second wave** breaks in **August and September 1692**, with 174 and 183 accusations. Outbreaks rarely end in a straight line.

Epidemiologists know this shape well: rise, peak, decline, resurgence. Paired with the loop-free network below it, the Salem record is a case study in **how things spread**, with data that classic epidemiological models are built to fit.

The graph has hubs, a recruitment mechanism, and no loops. Together they are why a set of courtroom accusations from 1692 can be analyzed with the mathematics of contagion. What looks like a tangle is, on inspection, a network pointing in exactly one direction.
